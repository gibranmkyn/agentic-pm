# Snap Strategy

A hypothesis-first approach to product strategy development. Write your best understanding of the strategy in 1-2 hours from what you already know, share it before it's polished, and iterate as evidence arrives. The McKinsey narrative-first methodology applied to product strategy.

**Author:** Ed Bayes — *How to Build a Snap Product Strategy* (2024).

---

## The core insight

Traditional bottom-up strategy development — gather data, then synthesize — is inefficient because it generates mountains of undirected data without a framework for interpretation. Hypothesis-first strategy development works in reverse:

1. Write the narrative: "This is true, therefore, conclusion"
2. Identify what would need to be true for each claim to hold
3. Hunt for evidence to validate or disprove each hypothesis
4. Update the narrative when evidence disproves a hypothesis

This produces far more focused research, faster learning, and strategies that trace back to evidence.

---

## The snap strategy format

A one-pager covering five elements:

1. **Objective** — what problem are we trying to solve? (qualitative, inspiring)
2. **Success metrics** — how do we define winning? (quantitative)
3. **Key pillars** — 3-5 main themes of work
4. **Evidence** — what backs each pillar, or where evidence needs to be gathered
5. **Outstanding risks** — what could prove this wrong?

Total time to first draft: 1-2 hours. Do not research before writing. Write from what you already know.

---

## Why writing first beats researching first

- A 1-hour draft is ~80% of a week-long draft, which is itself ~80% of the "perfect" version
- The gaps in the draft become a *targeted* research agenda — much more efficient than open-ended data gathering
- Sharing a draft surfaces what others know that you don't — generating new insights and surfacing disagreements early
- Disagreements with the draft are opportunities to learn, not failures

---

## Evolution: from doc to deck to vision

The strategy document evolves over time:

- **Phase 1: Word document** — just write. Don't worry about visuals or layout. This is the thinking phase.
- **Phase 2: Slide deck** — more visual, easier for stakeholders to consume. Charts, frameworks, structured argument with evidence.
- **Phase 3: Visual narrative** — mockups, wireframes, storyboards showing the future state product experience. Before/after comparisons.

The best strategy communications show a before/after: here's the current user journey, here's the future state.

---

## How to apply it

1. **Block 2 hours.** No research beforehand. Open a blank document.
2. **Write the five elements** from what you already know. Flag uncertainty explicitly: "I believe X but have no data."
3. **Share immediately** — with your manager, your skip-level, a peer. The draft is a conversation starter, not a polished deliverable.
4. **Collect reactions.** What do readers agree with? What do they challenge? What do they know that you don't?
5. **Build a targeted research plan** from the gaps the draft surfaced.
6. **Evolve** from doc to deck to visual narrative as evidence arrives and the audience expands.

---

## Common failure modes

| Failure | Fix |
|---|---|
| Researching for weeks before writing | Write first; research fills gaps the draft surfaces |
| Treating the first draft as precious | It's a hypothesis, not a position paper; expect 40% to change |
| No explicit risks section | If you can't name what could prove you wrong, the strategy hasn't been stress-tested |
| All pillars, no evidence | Each pillar needs either supporting evidence or a flagged gap |
| Skipping the evolution from doc to deck | The doc is for thinking; the deck is for communicating; the visual narrative is for selling |

---

## When NOT to use it

- When you genuinely know nothing about the space. The snap strategy assumes you have baseline domain knowledge to write from. If you have zero context, do a rapid orientation first.
- When the audience needs a formal deliverable on day one. The snap strategy is a draft-first methodology — it requires a culture that accepts work-in-progress.
