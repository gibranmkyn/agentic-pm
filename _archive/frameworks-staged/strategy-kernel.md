# Strategy Kernel

Most "strategies" are not strategies. They are lists of goals, vague aspirational slogans, or financial targets with no explanation of how you get there. Richard Rumelt's kernel is the minimum structure that separates real strategy from performance theater.

A real strategy has three parts: a diagnosis of what is actually hard, a guiding policy for how to deal with it, and coherent actions that address it directly. Any strategy document missing one of these is incomplete.

**Author:** Richard Rumelt — *Good Strategy Bad Strategy* (Crown Business, 2011); *The Crux* (PublicAffairs, 2022). See [richardrumelt.com](https://richardrumelt.com).

---

## Structure

### 1. Diagnosis

An honest articulation of what is actually hard about the situation — not what you wish were true, not a restatement of values. The diagnosis names the nature of the challenge and what makes it difficult.

- Bad diagnosis: "We're not growing fast enough." (That's a desire, not a diagnosis.)
- Good diagnosis: "Our onboarding conversion is 12% against an industry norm of 35%, and qualitative research shows users abandon during the third setup step because the required configuration is unclear."

Most organizational debates about strategy are actually unresolved debates about diagnosis. Align on the diagnosis before discussing what to do.

### 2. Guiding policy

The directional commitment for *how* to deal with the challenge identified in the diagnosis. The operative concept here is **focus**: the guiding policy concentrates resources rather than spreading them.

- A list of 17 priorities is not a guiding policy — it has no focus.
- A guiding policy doesn't have to be a complete plan. It's a choice about *how to approach* the challenge — which forces you to say no to other approaches.

### 3. Coherent actions

The specific things you will actually do. They must:
- Directly address the diagnosis and guiding policy
- Reinforce each other rather than cancel each other out
- Be expressed as **verbs, not adjectives** — concrete things someone will do, not qualities to embody

Example of incoherent actions: "We'll grow and increase margins." Growth and margin often trade off. Coherent actions force that resolution rather than dodging it.

---

## How to apply it

1. **Write the diagnosis first.** Not the goals, not the vision — the diagnosis. What is actually hard? Why is it hard?
2. **Test it with a critic.** If someone who disagrees with your strategy can read the diagnosis and say "yes, that's the problem," your diagnosis is real.
3. **Write the guiding policy as a choice.** Name what it rules out as explicitly as what it prioritizes.
4. **Audit the actions.** For each proposed action, ask: does this directly address the diagnosis? Does it reinforce the other actions, or fight them?
5. **Remove the adjectives.** Replace "improve team quality" with "hire two senior engineers in the first 90 days and replace the contractor arrangement by Q3."

## Common failure modes

| Failure | What it looks like | Why it's a problem |
|---|---|---|
| Goals masquerading as strategy | "We will be the market leader in X by 2027" | No diagnosis, no policy, no coherent actions — just ambition |
| Word salad | "Synergistic cross-functional excellence" | Sounds strategic; commits to nothing |
| 17 priorities | A balanced scorecard with 12 equal initiatives | No focus means no trade-offs; everything moves at walking pace |
| Missing diagnosis | Great-sounding policy with no problem it's solving | Teams can't evaluate whether actions are working |
| Adjective actions | "Build better engineering culture" | Nobody knows what to do tomorrow |

## When NOT to use it

- Early-stage discovery before you've diagnosed the market — the kernel assumes you have enough data to name the challenge. Build that first.
- For communicating strategy to a board or all-hands — the kernel is a thinking tool; external audiences often want a narrative layer on top (see [Strategic Narrative](strategic-narrative.md)).
