# LNO Framework

A task categorization framework for product managers that classifies every work item as Leveraged, Neutral, or Overhead. Created by Shreyas Doshi. The insight isn't the categories — it's what the categories force you to notice: most PM time disappears into Neutral and Overhead work, not because those things aren't real, but because there's no explicit system for protecting Leveraged time.

**Author:** Shreyas Doshi — former PM at Google, YouTube, Twitter, Stripe. See [shreyasdoshi.com](https://shreyasdoshi.com) and [@shreyas](https://x.com/shreyas) on X.

---

## The three categories

**Leveraged (L)**
Tasks that create disproportionate impact. Not just important — specifically those where full effort creates significantly more value than adequate effort would. The defining question: "If I spent 10x as long on this, would the outcome be dramatically better?" If yes, it's Leveraged. These are the 10x tasks.

Examples: defining the product strategy for H2; setting the quality bar for a new AI feature; deciding which customer opportunity to pursue; writing the PRD for the company's most important bet this quarter.

**Neutral (N)**
Tasks that need to get done and have standard impact. The work is real; giving it adequate effort is the right call. The defining question: "Would 10x effort on this meaningfully change the outcome?" If not, it's Neutral — do it well but don't over-invest.

Examples: weekly status updates; responding to reasonable stakeholder questions; attending most team ceremonies; standard feature scoping for non-critical items.

**Overhead (O)**
Tasks with little or no impact on the outcomes that matter. These drain time without adding value proportional to the effort. The right move: say no, delegate, deprioritize, or simply don't do them.

Examples: meetings you're attending but not contributing to or learning from; reports nobody reads; feature requests that don't connect to any current OKR; process work that exists because it always has.

## The problem LNO solves

PMs face a never-ending list of things they *could* do. Most of them are individually reasonable. None of them are individually removable. But collectively, they consume the entire week — leaving no time for the Leveraged work that actually determines whether the product succeeds.

Without an explicit classification, Leveraged work gets treated the same as Neutral work. Both get adequate effort. The Leveraged work needs more than adequate. The Neutral work needs exactly adequate. The Overhead work needs nothing.

LNO makes the distinction visible so you can act on it.

---

## How to apply it

**Weekly review:** Go through your upcoming calendar and task list. Classify each item L, N, or O.

Look at the distribution:
- If most things are N and O, that's a signal about where your time is going, not about the work that matters.
- If Leveraged items are crowded into late Friday or skipped entirely, something structural needs to change.

**For Leveraged items:** Block protected time. Do not let N work crowd it out. Give them the full effort the "10x question" tells you they deserve.

**For Neutral items:** Do them well. Do not over-invest. "Good enough" is the right bar — spending extra hours here has roughly zero marginal impact.

**For Overhead items:** Eliminate, delegate, or say no. The hardest part is identifying them — they often feel important in the moment. Apply the "if I didn't do this, what bad thing would actually happen?" test.

## Where LNO fits in stakeholder management

A PM's most common failure mode in stakeholder management is getting captured by N and O work: responding to every request, attending every meeting, updating every stakeholder proactively on every status. All of these are reasonable, none of them are Leveraged.

Using LNO to protect cross-functional coordination time — the relationship-building, data-sharing, and proactive alignment that is actually Leveraged — is the mechanism for turning stakeholder management from a time sink into a career accelerator.

Aakash Gupta's recommended technique: use LNO to ensure that proactive data sharing with stakeholders (a Leveraged activity) doesn't fall off the plate when execution pressure peaks.

## Career implications

LNO's value changes as PMs move up:

- **IC PM:** The L tasks are mostly craft — writing sharp PRDs, running tight discovery sessions, making good scope decisions
- **Lead PM/Director:** The L tasks shift toward people — which teammate do I invest in? Which stakeholder do I bring in early?
- **VP/CPO:** The L tasks are almost entirely structural — which bet does the company make? What strategy change do we need?

What's Leveraged at one career level is Neutral at the next. Recalibrate the classification whenever you change roles.

## Common failure modes

| Failure | What goes wrong |
|---|---|
| Classifying everything as N | Nothing is explicitly Leveraged; everything gets mediocre effort |
| Never saying no to O | Schedule fills with work that produces zero outcome movement |
| Using LNO to feel productive | The framework is for choosing where to give full effort, not for labeling your busyness |
| Forgetting to protect L time | Classified things correctly but didn't block time; N work crowds in anyway |
| Other-imposing the framework | Telling teammates their requests are "overhead" without context destroys trust; use LNO privately, not as a dismissal tool |

---

## When NOT to use it

- For feature prioritization within a roadmap. LNO is for personal time allocation, not for ranking product bets. Use DRICE or ICE for that.
- In a crisis mode where everything genuinely is urgent. LNO assumes a normal operating cadence with discretionary time. In a crisis, triage differently.
- As a justification for avoiding hard N work. Some Neutral work is just unglamorous. LNO is not a reason to drop it.
