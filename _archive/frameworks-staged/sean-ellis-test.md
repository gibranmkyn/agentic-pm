# Sean Ellis Test

A single survey question that answers the hardest question in early product work: do we have product-market fit? "How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?" Three choices: Not disappointed / Somewhat disappointed / Very disappointed.

If 40% or more of surveyed users say "very disappointed," the product likely has PMF. Below that threshold, you're optimizing before the foundation is in place.

**Author:** Sean Ellis — survey methodology published 2009; *Hacking Growth* (Crown Business, 2017, co-authored with Morgan Brown). See [seanellis.me](https://seanellis.me).

---

## How to run it

**Who to survey:** activated users — people who have experienced the core value, not just signed up. If you survey users who never got past onboarding, the signal is noise.

**Sample size:** 30 minimum for any signal; 100+ for confidence. Segment by acquisition channel, cohort, and usage level before drawing conclusions from the overall number.

**Follow-up questions:** ask users who say "very disappointed" what they would use instead, and what the main benefit is. Their language becomes your positioning and your acquisition messaging.

---

## Interpreting results

**40%+ "very disappointed":** PMF is likely. Focus shifts to acquisition, expansion, and retention. The work is no longer "do we have the right product?" but "how do we grow it?"

**Below 40%:** Don't scale. Find the segment that is very disappointed — there's almost always a smaller cohort with a much higher score. Who are they? What's different about their situation?

The "very disappointed" cohort is the bullseye: understand them deeply, build more of the product for them, and rewrite your messaging to attract more people who look like them.

**"Somewhat disappointed" users:** the highest-leverage near-term product opportunity. What would it take to move them to "very disappointed"? This often surfaces the 2–3 product improvements that most expand the PMF cohort.

---

## What it is and isn't

The 40% threshold is empirically derived from observing many companies, not theoretically grounded. It's a focusing mechanism and a go/no-go gate — not a precise scientific measurement.

False positives exist: high scores can be driven by switching costs (users hate leaving) rather than genuine love. Corroborate with retention cohorts, NPS, and word-of-mouth rates before treating the number as definitive.

Nubank calibrates to 40–50% for the Brazilian market, where survey responses trend more positive. Adjust for your context.

---

## Common failure modes

| Failure | Fix |
|---|---|
| Surveying unactivated users | Survey only users who've experienced the core value |
| Stopping at the aggregate number | The segment breakdown is where the insight lives |
| Scaling when you're at 35% | Don't. Scale amplifies what's already there, including the gap. |
| Treating the "somewhat disappointed" group as failures | They're your roadmap for expanding PMF |

---

## When NOT to use it

- Products with finite use cases (a one-time event tool) — the "if you could no longer use this" framing doesn't apply.
- Fewer than 30 activated users — too small for reliable signal.
- After PMF is clearly established — at that point, focus on growth metrics, not PMF measurement.
