# Opportunity Solution Tree (OST)

A visual framework that connects a **business outcome** to the **customer opportunities** that could move it, the **solutions** that address those opportunities, and the **assumption tests** that validate solutions before you commit to building.

**Author:** Teresa Torres — *Continuous Discovery Habits* (Product Talk, 2021). See [producttalk.org](https://www.producttalk.org).

---

## Structure

```
Business Outcome (what the team is responsible for moving)
 └── Opportunities (unmet needs surfaced from customer stories)
       └── Solutions (3–5 ideas per opportunity)
             └── Assumption Tests (smallest experiments that resolve the risk)
```

The tree enforces one rule: **you cannot place a solution without first naming the opportunity above it.** That rule is the whole point. Most teams jump from "I have an idea" to "let's build it" without naming the customer need the idea serves. The OST makes that jump visible.

---

## What each level means

**Business Outcome** — the metric the team is accountable for (e.g., "increase weekly active viewers", "reduce time-to-first-value"). Given to the team, not generated by it. A project or feature is not a business outcome.

**Opportunities** — unmet needs, pain points, or desires surfaced from **customer stories**, not brainstorming. An opportunity is a *customer experience moment* ("I can't remember the title my friend told me"), not a capability gap ("we don't have search history").

**Solutions** — ideas for how you might address a specific opportunity. Always force **3–5 solutions per opportunity** before committing. If there's only one solution under an opportunity, you're in the "one-idea funnel."

**Assumption Tests** — the smallest experiment that resolves the riskiest assumption under a solution. Not a full build. Often a prototype, a smoke test, or a conversation.

---

## How to apply it

1. **Start from the outcome.** If you don't have one, stop and get one. Do not start from ideas.
2. **Interview customers weekly.** Opportunities emerge from stories about specific recent experiences, not from surveys or "what would you like?" questions.
3. **Map 3–7 opportunity areas first** (breadth), then decompose the one(s) you're pursuing (depth). Working at all levels simultaneously creates incoherence.
4. **Force 3–5 solutions per opportunity** before committing to one.
5. **Name the riskiest assumption** under the chosen solution and design the smallest test that resolves it.
6. **Update the tree weekly** from 3–4 fresh interview snapshots. Don't wait for a quarterly synthesis.

---

## Common failure modes

| Failure | Fix |
|---|---|
| Solutions labeled as opportunities ("better search") | Ask: "If we built this, what customer experience would improve?" That experience is the opportunity. |
| Built once, never updated | Small weekly updates beat quarterly overhauls. |
| One solution per opportunity | Force 3–5 before picking one. |
| Opportunity space too wide (20+ items) | Pick 3–7 areas; let the edges be incomplete. |
| Skipped for "urgent" stakeholder requests | Reference the tree at every prioritization meeting. |

---

## B2B note

In B2B, buyer and end-user have different experiences and often conflicting incentives. Don't build one tree for "the customer" — build one tree per role (buyer team, end-user team), each rooted in its own outcome. The strategic context layer links them.

---

## AI-era addendum

AI doesn't change the OST structure. It changes speed:

- **Interview transcripts (Gong, Fathom, etc.)** surface patterns across many conversations — use as a supplementary signal, not a replacement for weekly human interviews.
- **Rapid prototyping (Lovable, Bolt, v0)** lowers the cost per assumption test — you can run more tests per week and probe riskier assumptions earlier.
- **AI synthesis warning** — AI summaries of interviews drop 20–40% of important detail. Update the OST from human review of key interviews, not AI synthesis alone.

When pressured to "add AI features," the OST provides discipline: name the opportunity the AI feature addresses. If it doesn't fit anywhere in the tree, either the tree is missing an opportunity or the feature request is noise.

---

## When NOT to use it

- You don't have a real outcome to work against (get one first).
- You have no access to customers and no path to getting access (fix that first).
- You're in a pure execution phase on an already-validated solution (the OST is a discovery tool).
