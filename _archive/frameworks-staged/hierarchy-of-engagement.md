# Hierarchy of Engagement

Most consumer products fail not because they lack features but because they try to build level 2 and level 3 on an unfound level 1. You can't buy your way out of a missing core action with growth loops or retention campaigns. The hierarchy is sequential — and it only moves in one direction.

**Author:** Sarah Tavel (Benchmark, ex-Pinterest) — *Hierarchy of Engagement* (Benchmark blog, 2018). See [benchmark.com](https://benchmark.com) and Sarah's writing.

---

## Three levels (sequential — must be climbed in order)

### Level 1 — Core action

The single action that, when a user completes it, makes them significantly more likely to return. This is the behavior your product exists to create.

**How to find it:** Analyze every action in your product. For each: what % of users complete it? And if they do, what's their probability of returning next week? The action with the highest return-correlation is your core action.

The North Star Metric should be a rate or volume of the core action — not downstream business metrics.

Examples: Facebook (early) → friending. Pinterest → pinning. Snapchat → snapping. Evernote → creating a note.

**Common mistake:** Building too many features before nailing the core action. Users don't know what they're supposed to do; the core action gets diluted; nobody forms a habit.

### Level 2 — Retention

The product gets better the more a user uses it. Accumulating value creates individual lock-in that compounds over time.

**The test:** Does using the product create more value in future sessions than in past sessions?

Examples: Pinterest → every pin enriches your personalized feed. Spotify → every listen improves your Discover Weekly. Evernote → every note increases the value of your searchable archive.

**The anonymity problem:** Products that let users stay anonymous can't accumulate individual benefits. Users who leave and come back find the same experience — no lock-in.

Level 2 is impossible to optimize if level 1 isn't nailed. Users who haven't formed a core action habit won't stick around long enough to experience accumulated value.

### Level 3 — Self-perpetuation

User actions in the product naturally generate new users or re-engage dormant ones. The product grows itself.

Examples: Pinterest → re-pinning sends a notification to the original pinner, re-engaging them. LinkedIn → profile completions prompt connection requests. WhatsApp → group chats pull in non-users.

**The failure mode:** Too much viral pressure destroys the experience. Houseparty and Clubhouse collapsed because viral growth created notification overload. Level 3 requires careful calibration.

---

## How to apply it

1. **Find your core action** using the retention-correlation analysis.
2. **Set your NSM** as the rate or volume of that core action.
3. **Audit your roadmap.** Strip out anything that doesn't improve core action completion, accumulation of value, or self-perpetuation — in that order.
4. **Use cohort analysis** to measure level progress: are new cohorts completing the core action more? Is the retention curve flattening? Are users bringing in other users?

---

## Common failure modes

| Failure | Fix |
|---|---|
| Building level 2 features without a clear level 1 | Run the retention-correlation analysis; find the core action first |
| Confusing engagement with retention | High engagement that doesn't predict return is the core action's output, not the core action itself |
| Vanity metrics hiding level failures | MAU and downloads don't show you whether the core action is working — switch to core-action-specific metrics |
| Over-engineered virality at level 3 | Virality that produces low-quality users damages the level 1 experience; focus on L1 first |

---

## When NOT to use it

- B2B products: levels 1 and 2 apply; level 3 looks different — referrals, multi-user workflows, and integrations replace social virality.
- Enterprise products with low natural usage frequency: the core action may be weekly or monthly, requiring different measurement cadences.
