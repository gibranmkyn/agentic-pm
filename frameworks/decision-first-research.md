# Decision-First Research

Most research fails not because of bad methods but because it was designed without a specific decision in mind. Findings accumulate. Nobody acts on them. The research budget gets questioned. The PM continues to rely on intuition.

Decision-first inverts the design order: clarify the decision before designing anything else. The research question, method, sample, and timeline all follow from the decision — not the other way around.

**Author:** Behzod Sirjani (Facebook, Slack) — *User Research Drives Strategy* (Reforge, 2023). See Reforge course materials.

---

## The inversion

**Decision-last (default):**
> Pick a method → collect data → hope findings drive something

**Decision-first:**
> Clarify the decision → identify the evidence gap → design research to close exactly that gap → decide

The wheel-of-fortune analogy: each research conversation is like placing a letter on the board. Decision-first ensures every letter reduces uncertainty about a specific known answer. Decision-last fills in random tiles and hopes the puzzle reveals itself.

---

## Three steps

### 1. Clarify the specific decision
Bound the question concretely. "What features should we prioritize to improve 30-day retention for our enterprise segment this quarter?" is a decision. "Why are users churning?" is a research area, not a decision.

Ask: if we had the research findings tomorrow, what would we decide that we can't decide today?

### 2. Identify the evidence gap
What information is currently missing that would change how you'd decide? Only research that closes this specific gap is worth doing. Everything else — however interesting — is a distraction.

### 3. Scope research to close the gap
Only after steps 1 and 2: pick the method, sample, and timeline. The decision determines the method; the method does not determine the decision.

---

## Three feasibility checks

Before committing to any research:

| Check | Question |
|---|---|
| **Feasible** | Can this actually be done given time, access, and resources? |
| **Reasonable** | Is the cost proportional to the stakes of the decision? |
| **Worthwhile** | Will the findings actually move us forward, or are we just checking a box? |

A research plan that fails any of these three checks should be redesigned, not executed.

---

## Why decision-last fails

Three failure modes:

1. **Wrong method** — without knowing the decision, teams default to surveys or interviews regardless of fit
2. **Wrong data** — even with the right method, teams collect the wrong data — interesting but not decision-relevant
3. **Wrong stakeholders** — without knowing the decision upfront, the people who need to act on the findings aren't involved in designing the research; buy-in is retroactive and weak

---

## How to apply it

1. **Get embedded in planning cycles early.** Ask: "What are the 2–3 things we could learn that would most change how we approach this roadmap?"
2. **Call out what you already know.** Before designing new research, surface existing knowledge across roles. Avoid redundant research.
3. **Write the decision explicitly in the research brief.** Every brief should begin: "We need to decide X. To decide well, we need to know Y."

---

## Common failure modes

| Failure | Fix |
|---|---|
| Research with no named decision | Write the decision before touching the method |
| Interesting findings that don't change anything | Trace back: what decision was this supposed to inform? If none, the research was designed backward |
| Stakeholders surprised by findings | Involve the people who will act on findings in the research design |

---

## When NOT to use it

- Early exploratory research where the goal is to discover what the right questions even are — in this mode, there may genuinely be no decision to clarify yet. Use decision-first once the problem space is rough-mapped.
- Continuous discovery (Torres) with a standing weekly cadence — the decision lens applies at the point of synthesis and prioritization, not to every interview.
