# Stakeholder Management

The practice of identifying, understanding, and managing relationships with people who have influence over — or interest in — your product decisions. One of the most consequential skills in product management. Every feature requires buy-in from engineering, design, legal, data, sales, and executives. The PM is the only person responsible for making that happen.

**Authors:** Aakash Gupta — *How to Say No as a PM* and *Politics in Product* (2023); Lenny Rachitsky — *Jury Duty Product Management* (2023). See [aakashg.com](https://www.aakashg.com) and [lennysnewsletter.com](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com).

---

## The core tension

Saying yes to everyone fails: features ship, metrics don't move, the roadmap becomes a stakeholder gift list. Saying no poorly also fails: political capital lost, resources pulled, working relationships damaged. The skill is navigating between these in a way that preserves trust while protecting the team's ability to work on what matters.

"How you process feedback determines your success as a PM." — Aakash Gupta

---

## Step 1 — Map the stakeholders

Before any alignment work, you need to know who the players are:

| Dimension | Categories |
|---|---|
| Interest level | High / Medium / Low |
| Influence level | High / Medium / Low |
| Current stance | Champion / Neutral / Skeptic / Blocker |

High influence + high interest = the critical path. These people can unblock or kill the initiative. They get the most engagement. Low influence + low interest = light-touch; a written update is enough.

Don't skip the "current stance" column. "Supportive" is not enough. You need to know what each stakeholder actually cares about — their incentive, their fear, their preferred outcome. That's what you're responding to in the alignment work, not the surface-level stance.

---

## Step 2 — Map the invisible org chart

The formal org chart misses who actually influences decisions. Three categories of informal influence to map:

- **Informal leaders** — respected people who shape opinion without title authority; when they say something is good, others follow
- **Power brokers** — people who control information flow, access, or resource allocation behind the scenes
- **Gatekeepers** — people who control access to decision-makers; getting past them is a prerequisite

You find informal leaders by watching who others defer to in meetings. You find power brokers by noticing who gets cc'd on everything. You find gatekeepers by noticing who controls the calendar.

---

## Step 3 — Name the objection for every critical stakeholder

This is the step that separates effective stakeholder management from the illusion of it. For every critical-path stakeholder, you must be able to answer: *What specifically might they object to or resist, and why?*

Vague: "The CTO is probably supportive."
Specific: "The CTO will push back on the timeline because she committed to shipping the infrastructure overhaul in Q3 and our feature shares the same team."

You can't respond to vague. You can only manage what's specific. If you don't know a stakeholder's objection, go find out before the big meeting. One 15-minute conversation is worth more than any presentation you could prepare.

---

## Step 4 — Pre-align before the big meeting

From Lenny Rachitsky: trial lawyers never ask a question they don't already know the answer to. All the persuasion work happens in depositions before the courtroom. Meetings should be "low-drama and full of non-surprises."

The product version: schedule 15-minute pre-meetings with every critical stakeholder before any high-stakes decision meeting. Use those conversations to:
- Share your position and hear their reaction privately
- Surface objections before the room hears them
- Adjust your proposal based on what you learn
- Give them the chance to feel consulted, not ambushed

"Meetings before the meeting" is not political theater. It's the only reliable way to walk into a high-stakes room knowing where the landmines are.

---

## Step 5 — Build the communication plan

What does each stakeholder need to hear, in what format, and at what cadence?

| Stakeholder type | Format | Cadence |
|---|---|---|
| Executive sponsor | Written summary, key decisions, outcome framing | Monthly or at milestones |
| Cross-functional partner | Informal sync, shared doc | Weekly during active work |
| Engineering lead | Direct 1:1, technical specifics | Weekly throughout |
| Skeptic | Structured conversation, evidence-first | Before each key decision |

The format matters. Some stakeholders read everything. Most read nothing and skim headlines. Calibrate to what each stakeholder actually consumes, not to what you'd prefer to send.

## Saying no effectively

The most misunderstood application of stakeholder management. Principles:

**Own the no.** "I don't believe we should prioritize that now because…" is better than "the data says we can't" or "leadership decided." Hiding behind frameworks or hierarchy damages trust. The no is yours — deliver it clearly.

**Never string stakeholders along.** A clear no is more respectful than weeks of "we're evaluating it." Stakeholders who don't know where they stand do more damage by continuing to lobby.

**Time it right.** A no delivered a week after the initial conversation — when emotional urgency has faded — often lands better than an immediate refusal.

**Always close in writing.** A verbal no leaves room for "I thought you were considering it." A written follow-up creates a clear record and forces you to articulate your reasoning.

**Explore non-product alternatives.** Sometimes a stakeholder's underlying need can be met without building anything. Ask "what outcome are you actually trying to create?" before treating every request as a build request.

## Managing political archetypes

Three behavior patterns to recognize and respond to:

**Matchers (majority):** Trade favors for favors. Be a strategic matcher — give generously, but track that reciprocity exists. Don't assume good intent guarantees they'll speak for you in the room.

**Restrictors:** Subtly undermine peers competing for the same promotion or resources. Recognize this pattern early; it looks like "helpful feedback" until it happens repeatedly. Protect your contributions publicly and build alliances that don't run through them.

**Thieves:** Take credit for others' work. Speak up clearly and early about your own contributions. Documentation protects you here.

## Influence = evidence + trust

From Lenny Rachitsky: a jury decides based on evidence quality and witness credibility — not attorney charisma. The losing side often has better theater.

When you struggle to align a stakeholder, diagnose which axis is failing:
- **Evidence weak?** → get the data, run the research, find the precedent
- **Trust low?** → find out why; repair it through delivery, transparency, or direct conversation

These have different remedies. Misdiagnosing means you bring data to a trust problem, or repair trust when what's missing is evidence.

## Building alliances proactively

Three practices that compound:

1. **Share data and customer insights proactively** — don't wait to be asked. When stakeholders receive interesting analytics or customer stories from you, they associate you with valuable information. This is the fastest trust-builder.
2. **Protect cross-functional coordination time** — use LNO to ensure this doesn't fall off when execution pressure peaks. Relationship-building is Leveraged work.
3. **Maintain a contact list and reach out regularly** — cold outreach to people you haven't talked to in a while is the most underrated relationship maintenance tool.

## Common failure modes

| Failure | What goes wrong |
|---|---|
| Skipping the pre-alignment 1:1s | You walk into the big meeting blind; the first time you hear the objection is in front of everyone |
| Classifying every stakeholder as "supportive" | You haven't named their specific interest or fear; you don't actually know |
| Under-explaining the no | PMs are busier than stakeholders; calibrate to their experience, not yours |
| No written follow-up on decisions | "I thought we were still considering it" reopens closed conversations |
| Alignment work only during crises | Relationships built under pressure don't survive; build them before you need them |

---

## When NOT to use it

- When the decision is in your domain and doesn't require broader buy-in. Not every decision needs a stakeholder plan — only the ones where someone outside your team can block or undermine the work.
- As a substitute for having a good decision. Stakeholder management can smooth communication of a decision, but it cannot save a weak one from scrutiny.
