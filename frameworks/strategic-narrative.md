# Strategic Narrative

A company's strategic narrative is its foundational story — the account of a world-change and the company's role helping customers navigate it. It is upstream of everything: positioning, product copy, sales decks, recruiting pitches. When it works, customers don't just buy a product; they join a movement.

Strategic narrative is distinct from product positioning. Positioning answers "why us over the alternative." Narrative answers "why does this category even matter right now, and why would someone change at all?"

**Author:** Andy Raskin — widely-read practitioner and consultant. See [andyraskin.com](https://andyraskin.com). Notable piece: *The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen* (2016, public Medium article).

---

## Structure (five steps)

### 1. Name the paradigm shift
"The world has changed from X to Y." Not "our product is better" — the observation that the rules of the game have changed. This is the most important step. Get this wrong and nothing else lands.

The shift must be real, verifiable, and already underway. If it's aspirational, prospects won't recognize themselves in it.

### 2. Define the stakes
Those who adapt to the shift win. Those who don't, lose. This is urgency without selling — you're naming what's at stake for the customer, not for you. If the stakes aren't genuine, the narrative becomes hype.

### 3. Describe the promised land
The new world your customer inhabits after making the change. Critically: the promised land exists whether or not your product exists. This is a destination, not a product description. Customers have to want the destination before they want your product.

### 4. Identify obstacles
The reasons people haven't reached the promised land yet — the problems that make the journey hard. Name these before introducing your solution. This step makes the product feel inevitable rather than convenient.

### 5. Present magic gifts
Your product's capabilities as the gifts that address the obstacles — in the same order the obstacles were presented. The product arrives as the answer to specific difficulties, not as a starting point.

---

## How to apply it

1. **Start with the CEO in the room.** Strategic narrative is a leadership artifact. If the CEO doesn't personally own it, it won't propagate through sales, marketing, recruiting, or product. Marketing-owned narrative gets ignored by everyone else.
2. **Write the shift first.** "The world has changed from __ to __." Test it: can a skeptical prospect agree the shift is happening even if they haven't decided to respond to it yet?
3. **Do two sessions.** The first surfaces what the team *thinks* they believe. The second confronts the contradictions and gaps. Most teams abandon the process at session two — the teams that push through produce a real narrative.
4. **Separate the narrative from the deck.** The narrative is the spine; the deck is one expression of it. Build the narrative first, then let it drive the deck, the website, the job postings.

---

## Common failure modes

| Failure | Why it breaks |
|---|---|
| Starting with the product | Customers tune out before reaching the shift — they have no reason to care yet |
| Aspirational shift that isn't happening yet | Prospects can't recognize themselves in it; narrative feels speculative |
| Promised land = product features | The destination collapses into a product description; loses the sense of movement |
| Narrative owned by marketing | Sales uses their own script; product uses another; recruiting improvises; each customer gets a different story |
| Stopping after session one | The real narrative work happens when you face the contradictions; session one surfaces what's comfortable, not what's true |

## When NOT to use it

- You're writing a positioning statement for a specific product within a category (use Dunford's method — see [Product Positioning](product-positioning.md)).
- The company is in execution mode with no need to sell the "why now" — an internal strategy kernel (see [Strategy Kernel](strategy-kernel.md)) is more useful.
