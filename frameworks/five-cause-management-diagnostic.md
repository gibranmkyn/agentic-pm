# 5-Cause Management Diagnostic

When something isn't getting done, most managers reach for the nearest explanation — usually motivation or attitude. Most of the time, that's wrong. There are five possible causes, ordered by how often they actually apply. Work through them in order. Stop when you find the real one.

Getting the right cause matters because the fixes are different. Motivation training doesn't fix a communication problem. Removing blockers doesn't fix a skill gap.

**Author:** Alex Hormozi — YouTube (2024). See [youtube.com/alexhormozi](https://www.youtube.com/alexhormozi).

---

## The five causes (in diagnostic order)

| Order | Cause | Diagnostic question | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Communication** | Did they know what you wanted? | State it clearly; write it down |
| 2 | **Training** | Do they know *how* to do it? | Teach the specific skill — see below |
| 3 | **Deadline** | Is there a time frame? | Set an explicit deadline |
| 4 | **Motivation** | Is there a reason to care? | Add reinforcement — incentive or consequence |
| 5 | **Blockers** | Is something in the way? | Identify and remove the obstacle |

---

## How to apply it

Ask the person directly. "Did you know I wanted you to do X?" → if no, cause 1. "Do you know how to do X?" → if no, cause 2. Work down the list. **Stop at the first "no."** Don't apply all five fixes simultaneously — you won't know what worked, and you'll create an overcorrection that makes the next delegation harder.

Causes 1, 3, and 5 are typically one-sentence fixes. Causes 2 and 4 take real work.

**Training (cause 2)** requires skill decomposition: you can't tell someone to "improve their communication" or "be more proactive." You have to unpack the specific observable behaviors that make up the skill — what would the fully competent version of this actually look like in practice? Then teach those behaviors, not the label.

**Motivation (cause 4)** requires understanding what happens after the person acts. Humans repeat behaviors that produce immediate, reliable reinforcement. If the work has no natural feedback loop — a delayed payoff, mostly negative interactions — you need to engineer tighter, more immediate reinforcement. Shorter cycles, visible progress, immediate acknowledgment.

---

## What this is not

The diagnostic applies to **behavior** (why isn't someone doing something). It doesn't apply to reasoning or decisions — for those, you need a different analysis tool.

It also doesn't apply when the problem is with **your** expectations rather than their execution. Before running the diagnostic, check: was the request reasonable given their other obligations and capacity?

---

## Common failure modes

| Failure | Why it happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Jumping to motivation | Motivation feels like the "real" cause; communication is too obvious | Work the list in order; don't skip |
| Fixing all five simultaneously | Efficient-seeming but doesn't isolate the cause | One fix at a time |
| Confusing abdication with delegation | Delegating without covering causes 1–5 upfront creates the problem you're diagnosing | Run the checklist before delegating, not after it fails |

---

## When NOT to use it

- When the issue is a pattern across multiple people — that's usually a process or system problem, not an individual behavior problem.
- When the person doesn't have the authority to remove their own blockers (cause 5) — address the structural issue first.
